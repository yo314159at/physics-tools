A ping-pong ball is at the mouth of an inverted funnel. Why does fast air blowing upward keep the ball from falling out?

Show the answer

Moving air in the gap has a lower effective pressure than the still air around the ball. That pressure difference plus the funnel walls pushes the ball back toward the constricted region.

  • Increase the airflow. The ball's equilibrium position moves farther out while gravity and the pressure restoring force balance.
  • Try pulling the ball away from the funnel. It is returned toward the constriction in the inverted orientation.
  • Flip the funnel upright and release the ball. Gravity no longer has to be balanced by the trapping pressure.
Common mistake. The ball is not held by a simple upward pushing jet alone. The confined fast flow lowers pressure in the gap, and the surrounding pressure also helps push it back toward the funnel.

For steady, approximately incompressible flow, Bernoulli compares pressure and flow speed:

$$p+\tfrac12\rho v^2+\rho gh=\text{constant}$$

In this demonstration the local pressure difference is represented by an effective force. At $v=10\,\text{m/s}$ in air ($\rho=1.20\,\text{kg/m}^3$),

$$\Delta p=\tfrac12\rho v^2=\tfrac12(1.20)(10^2)=60\,\text{Pa}$$

This is an educational effective-force model, not a full 2D airflow calculation. It assumes approximately steady incompressible air and idealizes wall forces and leakage.

  1. In the upside-down position, set the airflow low and scrub until the ball reaches the funnel mouth.
  2. Raise the airflow to 12 m/s, drag the ball outward, and let go. How far does it settle?
  3. Flip the funnel upright. What happens when the airflow remains on?
  4. Open the Pressure graph and compare $v$ and $\Delta p$; by what factor does pressure change if speed doubles?
